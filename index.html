Stage 1 · Prenatal development and infancy · Conception to 2 years

My beginning

I was born on September 17, 2007, at Mother Teresa of Calcutta Medical Center in San Fernando City, Pampanga. I am the third of four siblings, and I arrived earlier than expected: about seven months into the pregnancy, after a sudden medical emergency. That unexpected start was the first chapter of my developmental journey. What I know about my mother's pregnancy is limited, so I leave it as unknown.

My mother says I was crawling at eight months and soon pulling myself up to take small steps while holding onto nearby objects. I smiled a lot, a habit I still have. My first recorded word was “Daning,” the name my siblings and I use for our mother.

My parents and older siblings took turns looking after me. Their steady presence gave me a sense of security, which I now see as the foundation of a secure attachment. Relatives remember a cheerful, expressive baby who babbled and made sounds to everyone, and who rarely cried when different people carried me.

  • Born at about seven months of gestation
  • Crawling at eight months, then pulling up and taking small steps
  • First word: “Daning”
  • A cheerful, babbling baby who felt secure with many people
Stage 2 · Early and middle childhood

My childhood

One of my earliest memories is my first birthday at our family home. There were many visitors, mostly cousins and relatives, and I was carried from one family member to another all through the celebration.

By age 3 I had a basic understanding of English, thanks to my interest in books. Our home had reading materials about animals, astronomy and interesting facts, and they pushed me to explore on my own. By age 4 I could speak clearly, and I learned these early language skills through my own curiosity, without direct help from anyone.

School began with an embarrassing moment: I accidentally wet my shorts in class. I felt shy, and it dented my confidence in talking to classmates. In Grade 1, I was no longer with the classmates I knew from Kindergarten, so I started approaching others and beginning friendships instead of letting shyness decide for me. My cheerful, friendly nature helped.

Reading was my favorite hobby. I also taught myself the electric keyboard through exploration and practice, until I stopped around Grade 5. I can no longer play it, but I remember it as proof of my curiosity and willingness to learn new skills.

Being friendly came with a lesson. My enthusiasm could sometimes overwhelm others, even when I never meant to make anyone uncomfortable. I learned that not everyone returns the same energy, and that good relationships need understanding, consideration and respect for personal boundaries.

  • Age 3: basic English through books about animals and astronomy
  • Age 4: speaking clearly, self-taught
  • Kindergarten: an embarrassing day that made me shy
  • Grade 1: taking the first step to make new friends
  • Teaching myself the electric keyboard until Grade 5
Stage 3 · Adolescence

My teenage years

I was relatively short as a child and grew taller as I entered adolescence. It was one of the clearest physical changes marking my move from childhood into my teenage years.

I became more aware of my emotions and of how my actions affected people around me. My thinking grew more reflective. I formed stronger opinions, questioned certain beliefs, and considered other perspectives before deciding. There were moments of uncertainty and mood changes, but they gave me more self-awareness and maturity.

My friendships became more meaningful as I learned to value genuine connections and accept that not everyone would stay in my life. My family became a source of guidance while I took on more independence and responsibility for my decisions. School brought new academic expectations that made me more adaptable and responsible.

Family and peers shaped my decisions and values. Their opinions made me reconsider some choices, and also showed me that I did not always have to follow other people's expectations. I began to ask who I wanted to become, what truly mattered to me, and whether my beliefs were really my own.

I am proud of the academic excellence awards I earned, because they reflected the time and effort I put into my studies. As I grew older, though, I felt academic burnout and began to question my constant chase for recognition. Awards and grades should not be the only measure of my worth. That became a turning point: I learned to value learning, well-being and growth beyond achievements.

  • A growth spurt after a short childhood
  • More reflective thinking and stronger personal opinions
  • Academic excellence awards
  • Academic burnout, and a turning point in how I measure my worth
Stage 4 · My present self · Emerging adulthood

Who I am now

Emerging adulthood fits me best right now: a period of growing independence, self-discovery and thinking about what comes next. I am a second-year psychology student from Pampanga, learning to take responsibility for my decisions while I explore my values, interests and long-term goals. In my first year of college I was included in the Dean's List.

College taught me to manage my time and balance schoolwork with my personal life. I make more of my own decisions and handle my own expenses, and that has made me more independent, disciplined and accountable. My relationships with family and friends have stayed steady. My responsibilities have changed, but the people who support me matter just as much.

I see myself as curious, expressive and growth-oriented. I enjoy reading, writing and exploring ideas. My strengths are creativity, adaptability and a willingness to understand different perspectives, and I value authenticity, self-awareness and the freedom to grow at my own pace.

I do not have a clear career goal yet. For now I want to finish my psychology degree, gain more experience and find the path that suits me. I believe I still have time to figure it out.

My biggest challenge is academic pressure: many deadlines and high expectations of myself can leave me overwhelmed and mentally exhausted. I cope by taking things one step at a time, resting when I need to, and reminding myself that I do not have to do everything perfectly.

  • Dean's List in my first year of college
  • Managing my own time and expenses
  • Reading, writing and exploring ideas
  • Finishing my degree and discovering my path

Developmental theories

Theories behind my story

Three theories help me understand my own experiences. Open one to read how it connects to my life.

Erik EriksonPsychosocial theory · Identity vs. Role Confusion+
The theory
Erikson's psychosocial theory explains how people develop their personality and sense of identity through different stages of life, each with its own psychosocial challenge.
My experience
In adolescence I began asking who I wanted to become, what truly mattered to me, and whether my beliefs reflected my own values. I also started to reconsider my pursuit of academic recognition after I felt exhausted from constantly aiming for excellence.
How it explains my development
These experiences connect to the stage of Identity vs. Role Confusion, which is about forming a clearer sense of identity in adolescence. At this stage people explore their beliefs, values, interests and goals to understand who they are and who they want to become. My experience helped me see that personal growth and self-worth should not rest entirely on academic achievements or other people's expectations. Gradually I gained more self-awareness and a clearer understanding of my identity (Branje et al., 2021).
Key term
Identity vs. Role Confusion
Jean PiagetCognitive development theory · Preoperational stage+
The theory
Piaget's theory explains how children's thinking and understanding develop as they grow and interact with their environment.
My experience
Around age 3, I became interested in books about animals, astronomy and interesting facts. Out of curiosity I explored these materials and gradually built a basic understanding of English through independent learning.
How it explains my development
This fits the preoperational stage, which generally runs from about age 2 to 7. In this stage children develop language and symbolic thinking, using words and symbols to represent ideas and make sense of their surroundings. My early interest in books shows how I explored information, built language skills and made sense of new concepts. It also shows how curiosity and contact with learning materials supported my early cognitive development (Scott & Cogburn, 2023).
Key term
Preoperational stage
Urie BronfenbrennerEcological systems theory · Microsystem+
The theory
Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory explains how a person's development is shaped by the different environments and relationships around them throughout life.
My experience
In infancy, my parents and older siblings shared in caring for me and meeting my needs. When I entered school, my interactions with classmates made me gradually more comfortable approaching people and building friendships.
How it explains my development
Both experiences show the microsystem: the immediate environments and relationships that directly affect development. My family gave me early care and support, while my school gave me chances to interact with others and build social skills. These everyday interactions shaped how I learned to communicate, form relationships and adjust to different social situations (Dimova et al., 2026).

Reflecting on the person I am becoming

The milestone that shaped me

Learning to build friendships in my early school years had the greatest impact on who I am today. I used to be shy and hesitant to approach other children, especially after the embarrassment I felt in Kindergarten. When I entered Grade 1, I decided to be more open and take the first step with my new classmates. That made me more confident, expressive and comfortable with different kinds of people. It also taught me that personal growth often begins when I step outside my comfort zone.

Who and what influenced me

My family, especially my parents and siblings, influenced me most, because they gave me care, guidance and support throughout my childhood. School mattered too: time with classmates and teachers made me more confident, sociable and responsible. Reading and exploring different topics early fed my curiosity and independent learning, and as I grew older, technology opened more ways to find information, discover new interests and widen my perspective.

How I have changed

Physically: I grew taller during adolescence after being relatively short as a child.

Cognitively: I became more curious and built independent learning habits, such as exploring books about animals and astronomy at an early age.

Emotionally: I became more self-aware and learned to manage my feelings, especially under academic pressure and personal expectations.

Socially: I became more confident approaching people, starting with the friendships I initiated in Grade 1.

What I have learned

My development was shaped by big milestones and also by ordinary experiences I once overlooked. Looking back at childhood, adolescence and today, I can see how much I have changed physically, cognitively, emotionally and socially. Some of my strengths, interests and values came from the people I met and the challenges I faced. Growth is not measured only by achievements, but also by the lessons I take from my experiences. I am still discovering who I am, and every stage has contributed to the person I am becoming.

Who I hope to become

I hope to be confident and secure in my own decisions, without constantly feeling the need to meet other people's expectations. One goal is to build greater self-confidence and trust my own abilities. I want to be more comfortable with uncertainty and accept that I do not need to have everything figured out right away. I also hope to stay open to learning and improving, and to make meaningful choices that follow my own values.

The theory that helped most

Erikson's psychosocial theory helped me understand myself best, especially the stage of Identity vs. Role Confusion. It made me realize that questioning my beliefs, values, interests and future goals is a natural part of building an identity. I noticed that my changing view of academic achievement and personal expectations in adolescence still shapes how I see myself. My identity is not defined only by what I accomplish or by what other people expect of me. It keeps developing as I gain new experiences, make decisions and find out what matters to me.
